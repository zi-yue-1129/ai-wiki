# Animal Cognition and Comparative Intelligence

Many animals solve problems that were once thought to be uniquely human. Some recognise themselves in a mirror, make tools, and predict what others know. Animal cognition asks which of these abilities are shared across species, which have evolved independently, and what they tell us about intelligence in general. Its central tool is the controlled experiment, which lets researchers test what an animal can do rather than what it looks like it does.

## Overview

Comparative cognition uses two main strategies. The first compares closely related species to trace how an ability evolved. The second compares distant species that share a similar ability, such as corvids and great apes, to ask whether intelligence has evolved more than once. Both strategies depend on careful controls, because an animal that appears to understand something may be using a simpler cue. [Psychology & AI](../psychology/index.md) covers the human side of the same questions, including theory of mind, and [Developmental Cognition](../developmental-cognition/index.md) asks how these abilities appear in human infants.

## Key concepts

- **Self-recognition**: the ability to recognise one's own reflection, usually tested with a mark placed on the body that the animal can see only in a mirror.
- **Tool use and tool making**: using an object to reach a goal, and modifying an object to make it more useful.
- **Causal understanding**: knowing how one event leads to another, as opposed to learning a fixed response.
- **Convergent evolution**: similar abilities arising in lineages that split long ago, such as corvids and primates.
- **Theory of mind**: tracking what another individual knows or believes, which is discussed in [the chimpanzee theory-of-mind test](../psychology/chimpanzee-theory-of-mind/index.md).

## How it works

Animal cognition experiments are designed to separate a target ability from simpler explanations. A mark test, for example, must rule out that the animal is simply reacting to a novel object, and a tool test must rule out trial-and-error learning. A common pattern is to introduce a new problem, record which strategies the animal uses, and then vary the problem to see whether the solution transfers.

## Applications

- **Animal welfare**: tests of self-recognition and cognition inform decisions about how animals are housed and treated.
- **AI evaluation**: comparative tests are used as benchmarks for whether machines show causal or social reasoning, though such comparisons need care.

## Open questions

- **Does a passed test mean the same thing across species?** A mirror mark or a tool task may depend on sensory and motor features that differ between animals.
- **How much of animal intelligence is convergent?** Similar abilities in distant lineages raise the question of whether they share mechanisms or only results.

## References

### Seminal papers

- Gallup, G. G. (1970). Chimpanzees: self-recognition. *Science*, 167(3914), 86–87. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.167.3914.86 — Introduces the mark test, in which chimpanzees touch a mark on their face that they can see only in a mirror.
- Weir, A. A. S., Chappell, J. & Kacelnik, A. (2002). Shaping of hooks in New Caledonian crows. *Science*, 297(5583), 981. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1073433 — Reports a captive crow shaping a straight wire into a hook to retrieve food.
- Krupenye, C., Kano, F., Hirata, S., Call, J. & Tomasello, M. (2016). Great apes anticipate that other individuals will act according to false beliefs. *Science*, 354(6308), 110–114. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf8110 — Uses eye tracking to show that apes expect another individual to search where it falsely believes an object to be.

### Surveys and reviews

- Emery, N. J. & Clayton, N. S. (2004). The mentality of crows: convergent evolution of intelligence in corvids and apes. *Science*, 306, 1903–1907. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1098410 — Argues that corvids and apes show similar cognitive abilities despite separate evolutionary histories.
- Anderson, J. R. & Gallup, G. G. (2011). Which primates recognize themselves in mirrors? *PLoS Biology*, e1001024. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1001024 — Reviews which primate species pass the mark test and what this pattern implies.
