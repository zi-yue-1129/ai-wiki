# Mirror Self-Recognition (Gallup, 1970)

Gallup (1970) introduced a simple test of whether an animal recognises itself in a mirror. A chimpanzee was given a mirror and, after a period of exposure, had an odourless mark placed on an area of its face it could see only in the reflection. Chimpanzees that touched the mark while looking at the mirror seemed to treat the reflection as their own body. The test became one of the most cited methods in comparative cognition, and it has been applied across many species.

This page is part of [Animal Cognition and Comparative Intelligence](../index.md). The parent page gives the overview; this page covers the method and its evidence.

## The problem

A mirror image is visually familiar, and an animal may react to it as to another animal, a threat or a novel object. Showing an animal its reflection does not, by itself, show that it knows the reflection is itself. The question was how to find a behaviour that only self-recognition would explain.

## The approach

The key move is a mark that the animal cannot see directly but can see in the mirror. If the animal touches the mark on its own body while looking at the mirror, it is using the reflection to find its body. The test controls for simpler explanations by comparing behaviour with the mark and without it.

The steps were:

1. Let the animal become familiar with a mirror over a period of exposure.
2. Place a mark on a part of the body the animal cannot see directly, such as the face or head.
3. Observe whether the animal touches the mark while looking at the mirror, and compare this with behaviour when the mark is absent.
4. Record whether the animal's responses to the reflection show self-directed behaviour, rather than social or threat responses.

Step 3 is the key contrast. Touching a mark that is only visible in the mirror is hard to explain without some link between the reflection and the animal's own body.

## Results

Gallup reported that chimpanzees touched the marked area while looking in the mirror, and that the behaviour was less common without the mark. Later studies applied the same logic to other species, including elephants and dolphins. The summary used for this page gives these results qualitatively. Check the original paper and later studies before quoting specific counts.

## Limitations

- The test depends on vision. Species that rely more on smell, sound or touch may fail it without lacking self-awareness.
- Passing the test in one species is not evidence that a species without a pass lacks self-recognition. Failures can reflect the method.
- Some species respond to a mark with grooming or other behaviour that is hard to classify, which makes the criteria contested.
- The test measures one form of self-recognition, not self-awareness in general.

## Open questions

- **Which species pass, and why?** The pattern of passes across species is hard to explain by phylogeny alone, and sensory and motor features may play a part.
- **Can the test be modified to be fairer?** Attempts to train or engineer self-recognition in species that fail the unaided test raise methodological questions.

### Seminal papers

- Gallup, G. G. (1970). Chimpanzees: self-recognition. *Science*, 167(3914), 86–87. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.167.3914.86 — Introduces the mark test, in which chimpanzees touch a mark on their face that they can see only in a mirror.
- Plotnik, J. M., de Waal, F. B. M. & Reiss, D. (2006). Self-recognition in an Asian elephant. *PNAS*, 103(45), 17053–17057. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0608062103 — Applies the mark test to an elephant, which touches a mark on its head seen only in a mirror.

- Anderson, J. R. & Gallup, G. G. (2015).
