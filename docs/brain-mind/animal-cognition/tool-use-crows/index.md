# Tool Use in New Caledonian Crows (Weir et al., 2002)

Weir, Chappell and Kacelnik (2002) reported that a captive New Caledonian crow shaped a straight piece of wire into a hook, which it then used to retrieve food. The result drew attention because tool use had been treated as rare outside primates, and because the bird shaped its tool rather than only picking one up. Subsequent work on New Caledonian crows has tested tool making, tool combination and causal understanding in more detail, making these birds one of the best-studied cases in comparative cognition.

This page is part of [Animal Cognition and Comparative Intelligence](../index.md). The parent page gives the overview; this page covers the method and its evidence.

## The problem

Tool use is widely seen as a sign of intelligence, but a single observed use can come from trial and error or from a fixed response. To claim that an animal understands a tool, researchers need to show that it modifies or combines objects in ways that fit a goal, and that it transfers this understanding to new situations.

## The approach

The work uses problems that separate understanding from trial and error. The crows face a food reward that is out of reach, with tools that must be shaped, combined or chosen to solve the problem. Later studies added a causal test based on the Aesop's fable paradigm, in which the crow drops objects into water to raise food within reach.

The steps were:

1. Present a captive crow with food that cannot be reached directly.
2. Provide a straight piece of wire or other material that can be shaped into a tool.
3. Record whether the crow bends the material into a hook and uses it to retrieve the food.
4. In later studies, test whether the crow chooses objects that would raise the water level, which requires understanding of displacement rather than trial and error.

Step 3 is the central contrast. A shaped hook that works on the first try is hard to explain as a chance result.

## Results

Weir and colleagues reported that the crow shaped a wire into a hook and used it for retrieval. Later work, including studies of wild crows, found variation in tool design across populations and tool combinations that go beyond a single tool. The summary used for this page gives these results qualitatively. Check the original papers before quoting specific trial counts or measurements.

## Limitations

- The original report is of a single captive bird, so generality across the species needs replication.
- Captive conditions, including the provision of materials, may differ from the wild.
- Good performance on one problem does not show that the crow understands tools in general. The transfer of understanding is tested only in some studies.
- Tool design in wild populations may depend on social learning, which is harder to separate from individual understanding.

## Open questions

- **Do crows understand causes or learn patterns?** Causal tests are only some of the evidence, and the difference between understanding and learned association is still debated.
- **How much tool design is culturally transmitted?** Variation across wild populations raises the question of how far it reflects social learning.

## References

### Seminal papers

- Weir, A. A. S., Chappell, J. & Kacelnik, A. (2002). Shaping of hooks in New Caledonian crows. *Science*, 297(5583), 981. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1073433 — Reports a captive crow shaping a straight wire into a hook to retrieve food.
- Taylor, A. H., Hunt, G. R., Holzhaider, J. C. & Gray, R. D. (2007). Spontaneous metatool use by New Caledonian crows. *Current Biology*, 17, 1504–1507. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.07.057 — Shows crows using one tool to obtain another tool, which can then be used to reach food.
- Jelbert, S. P., Taylor, A. H., Cheke, L. G., Clayton, N. S. & Gray, R. D. (2014). Using the Aesop's fable paradigm to investigate causal understanding of water displacement by New Caledonian crows. *PLoS ONE*. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0092895 — Tests whether crows understand water displacement by choosing objects that would raise the water level.

### Surveys and reviews

- Hunt, G. R. & Gray, R. D. (2003). Diversification and cumulative evolution in New Caledonian crow tool manufacture. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 270, 867–874. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2002.2302 — Documents variation in tool design across wild crow populations and discusses how tool designs change over time.
- Emery, N. J. & Clayton, N. S. (2004). The mentality of crows: convergent evolution of intelligence in corvids and apes. *Science*, 306, 1903–1907. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1098410 — Places crow tool use and causal reasoning in a comparative setting with apes.
