# Chimpanzee Theory-of-Mind Test (Premack & Woodruff 1978)

Premack and Woodruff asked whether a chimpanzee can attribute goals to a human actor. The chimpanzee watched videotapes of a person struggling with a problem, then chose between photographs of possible solutions. The paper introduced the term *theory of mind* for this capacity. It is the parent topic's first landmark method; see [Psychology & AI](../index.md).

## Problem

Can an animal infer what another agent is trying to do, or does it only respond to the visible action? A test has to separate goal attribution from simply reacting to what is seen.

## Core idea

If the chimpanzee reliably picks the photograph that solves the actor's problem, the authors argue that it understood the actor's goal and not only the movements it saw. The actor is shown as stuck, so the choice of solution reveals what the chimpanzee takes the actor to want.

## Procedure

1. Show an adult chimpanzee a series of videotaped scenes of a human actor struggling with a variety of problems.
2. Present several photographs of possible solutions, one of which solves the problem shown.
3. Record which photograph the chimpanzee chooses.
4. Infer goal attribution from a consistent choice of the correct photograph.

## Results

The abstract reports that the chimpanzee made a consistent choice of the correct photographs. It gives no trial counts or statistics, so the numbers have to be checked in the full paper.

## Limitations

- The study tests a single adult chimpanzee, so it cannot show how typical the result is.
- Simpler explanations, such as learned associations between scenes and pictures, have to be ruled out before goal attribution is accepted. Whether the design does this is still debated.
- The test concerns goals in one narrow setting. It does not separately test beliefs about the world, which later work on false belief addresses.

## References

### Seminal papers

- Premack, D. & Woodruff, G. (1978). Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind? *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00076512 — Introduces the term "theory of mind" and a videotape test of whether a chimpanzee attributes goals to a human actor.
- Rabinowitz, N. C. et al. (2018). Machine Theory of Mind. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07740 — ToMnet, a meta-learned network that models other agents' behaviour and passes a Sally–Anne false-belief test in gridworlds.
- Sap, M. et al. (2022). Neural Theory-of-Mind? On the Limits of Social Intelligence in Large LMs. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13312 — Finds that GPT-3 scores well below human accuracy on social and theory-of-mind questions.
- Ullman, T. (2023). Large Language Models Fail on Trivial Alterations to Theory-of-Mind Tasks. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08399 — Small changes that keep the same principles reverse reported LLM successes on theory-of-mind tasks.
- Shapira, N. et al. (2023). Clever Hans or Neural Theory of Mind? Stress Testing Social Reasoning in Large Language Models. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14763 — Adversarial tests show that LLM social reasoning is inconsistent and may rely on shallow heuristics.
