# Patients with medial temporal lobe lesions (Scoville & Milner, 1957)

Patients with bilateral removal of the medial temporal lobe show that this brain region is needed to form new long-term memories, while many other abilities survive.

## Overview

Some of the clearest evidence about how memory works comes from people whose brains were damaged. In 1953 a patient known as H.M. underwent bilateral medial temporal lobe resection to treat severe epilepsy. Afterwards he could hold a conversation and remember events from his earlier life, but he could not form new lasting memories of everyday events. William Scoville and Brenda Milner (1957) described this and other cases in detail, and their paper became the starting point for the modern study of memory systems.

The paper matters for two reasons. It showed that a specific brain region is necessary for forming new memories, and it showed that memory is not one faculty: some capacities are lost while others are spared. Later work built on these observations and linked them to the systems that the other pages in this section discuss.

## Key concepts

- **Medial temporal lobe**: the inner part of the temporal lobe, including the hippocampus and the neighbouring entorhinal, perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices. Squire and Zola-Morgan (1991) describe these structures as the medial temporal lobe memory system.
- **Anterograde amnesia**: loss of the ability to form new memories after the damage.
- **Retrograde amnesia**: loss of memories formed before the damage. In these patients it was partial and graded in time.
- **Declarative memory**: long-term memory for facts and events that can be consciously recalled. The hippocampal system is essential for forming it.
- **Immediate memory**: the capacity to hold a small amount of information for seconds or minutes, which relies on other systems.
- **Lesion study**: research that infers the function of a brain region from the deficits that follow its damage.

## Problem

Which brain structures are needed to form new long-term memories? Before these studies, memory was difficult to localise. Damage to the cortex often caused broad and inconsistent effects, and it was not clear whether memory depended on a particular region or was spread across the brain. Scoville and Milner asked which deficits follow from removing the medial temporal lobes, and which abilities are unaffected.

## Core idea

The method is to study patients whose surgery left a known lesion, and to test what they can and cannot remember. The 1957 paper reports ten patients, most with bilateral damage to the medial temporal lobe, and the best-known is H.M. The paper compares the severity of each patient's memory deficit with the extent of the lesion. Patients with the most extensive bilateral damage showed the most severe deficits, while those whose lesions spared the hippocampus, or were limited to one side, showed no persistent defect. The comparison allowed the authors to connect the memory impairment to a particular structure.

## Equation or algorithm

There is no equation. The method is a structured lesion study with memory tests, which can be written as steps:

1. Identify patients with a known surgical lesion and assess their general intelligence with a standard battery.
2. Test immediate memory, such as recall of digits, stories and drawings, to check whether short-term capacity is intact.
3. Test delayed recall and new learning, such as associate learning and recognition of tests already taken, to measure anterograde amnesia.
4. Test recent and remote memory, including recall of events from early life, to map the pattern of retrograde loss.
5. Compare the deficit with the extent and side of the lesion, and with the patients whose lesions spared the hippocampus.

## Results

The core finding is anterograde amnesia for new declarative material. Severely affected patients forgot new information once their attention moved on, and they did not improve on delayed recall or on learning associations. H.M. could not learn new word associations, and he could not recognise tests he had already taken.

Other abilities were relatively spared. Patients could hold a short sequence of digits or words for several minutes if they were not distracted, so immediate memory was largely intact. Remote memory from early life was preserved in detail. The retrograde loss was partial and graded in time: H.M. lost memories from the years before the surgery, but earlier memories remained. The authors also reported that technical skills were intact in these patients, which pointed to a dissociation between kinds of memory.

Later work strengthened this result. Vargha-Khadem et al. (1997) describe three children with early bilateral hippocampal damage who had global anterograde amnesia for episodes of everyday life, but who attended mainstream schools and reached average or low-average levels of language, literacy and factual knowledge. This supports the view that episodic and semantic components of memory are partly separable, and that only the episodic component fully depends on the hippocampus.

## Limitations

The sample is small, and the lesions were surgical and could not be controlled precisely. The patients differed in age, in their illnesses and in the exact extent of the removal, so the conclusions rest on a comparison rather than on a controlled experiment. The tests were those available at the time, and they may not capture all forms of memory.

The study shows that these structures are necessary for forming new declarative memories. It does not show how they store memories. It also cannot separate the hippocampus from the neighbouring structures, because the surgical lesions removed them together. Animal studies, such as the monkey model reviewed by Squire and Zola-Morgan (1991), help to separate the contribution of each part. The mechanism of storage is addressed by work on [long-term potentiation](../long-term-potentiation/index.md) and by systems theories such as the [complementary learning systems](../complementary-learning-systems/index.md) framework.

## Key methods

Three landmark alternatives study the same question with different tools:

- Animal lesion studies: selective damage to parts of the medial temporal lobe in monkeys, which separates the contributions of the hippocampus and the neighbouring cortices (Squire & Zola-Morgan, 1991).
- Developmental lesion studies: patients with early hippocampal damage, which show how memory develops when the structure is damaged early (Vargha-Khadem et al., 1997).
- Neuroimaging of memory: measuring activity during encoding and recall in healthy people, which shows where memory-related activity occurs but not whether it is necessary. See [Neuroimaging Methods](../../neuroimaging-methods/index.md).

## Applications

- **Clinical assessment.** Tests that separate immediate, recent and remote memory, and that check new learning, are used to assess amnesia after injury, surgery or disease. The logic of these tests goes back to the cases described in the 1957 paper.
- **Teaching memory systems.** The case of H.M. is the standard example for separating declarative from non-declarative memory. Squire (2009) reviews what the case contributed to neuroscience.
- **Guiding theory.** The observations motivate systems theories, which propose that the hippocampus is a temporary store and that memory gradually becomes independent of it. Squire and Zola-Morgan (1991) state this view of a temporary role that fades over time.

## Open questions

- Which parts of the medial temporal lobe contribute to which memory functions, such as the hippocampus, the perirhinal cortex and the amygdala?
- How long does the hippocampus remain necessary for a memory, and what determines when it becomes independent?
- Do the patterns seen in adults and in children with early damage reflect the same underlying organisation?
- How do the patient findings relate to the replay and interleaving proposed in complementary learning systems?
