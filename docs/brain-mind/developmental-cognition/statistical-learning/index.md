# Statistical Learning in Infants (Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996)

Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) showed that eight-month-old infants can detect word-like units in continuous speech after only a few minutes of exposure. The infants heard a stream of syllables with no pauses or stress marking word boundaries. They then distinguished sequences that had high transition probabilities, meaning that one syllable reliably followed another, from sequences that did not. The result showed that infants extract structure from the statistics of their input, which is one of the foundations of the statistical-learning account of language acquisition.

This page is part of [Developmental Cognition and How Children Learn](../index.md). The parent page gives the overview; this page covers the method and its evidence.

## The problem

Speech arrives as a continuous sound, and infants must find where words begin and end before they know what the words mean. Early accounts relied on stress, pauses or the meaning of words, which are not always available. The question was whether the statistics of the sound stream alone could provide the segmentation cues infants need.

## The approach

Infants were familiarised with a continuous stream of syllables, generated from a few nonsense words strung together in random order. Within the stream, the syllables inside a word followed one another with high probability, while syllables across word boundaries did not. After a short exposure, the infants were tested on two sequences of syllables, one that had been a word in the stream and one that had crossed a boundary.

The steps were:

1. Play infants a continuous stream of syllables with no pauses or stress marking word boundaries.
2. Vary the transition probabilities so that syllables within a word follow one another reliably, and syllables across boundaries do not.
3. Test infants on a sequence that was a word in the stream and a sequence that spanned a boundary.
4. Compare listening times, to see which sequence the infants recognised as familiar.

Step 2 is what makes the statistics the only cue. The design removes stress and pauses, so any preference must come from the transition structure.

## Results

The paper reports that after a few minutes of exposure, the eight-month-old infants listened differently to the familiar word-like sequences than to the boundary sequences. The summary used for this page gives this qualitatively. Check the full paper before quoting specific values.

## Limitations

- The stream was artificial, with nonsense words and no meaning, so the generality to natural speech needs separate evidence.
- The task shows detection of statistical structure, not understanding of words or grammar.
- The effect size and its persistence over time depend on the details of the exposure and the test.

## Open questions

- **How far does statistical learning go?** It explains segmentation well, but whether it extends to grammar, meaning and abstract rules is contested.
- **Is statistical learning separate from core knowledge?** The two may use different mechanisms, and their interaction is still being studied.
