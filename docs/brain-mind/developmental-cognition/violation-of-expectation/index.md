# Violation of Expectation (Baillargeon, Spelke & Wasserman 1985)

Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman (1985) used looking time to show that five-month-old infants expect objects to continue to exist when they are hidden. Infants looked longer at a display in which an object seemed to pass through a solid barrier than at a possible event, which suggests that they register the impossibility. The paradigm let researchers ask what infants know without requiring them to reach, speak or point, and it became a standard method in developmental psychology.

This page is part of [Developmental Cognition and How Children Learn](../index.md). The parent page gives the overview; this page covers the method and its evidence.

## The problem

Piaget had argued that object permanence develops late in infancy, around the second year. If that were right, very young infants should not expect a hidden object to persist. But infants cannot report what they know, so an earlier competence could go undetected. The question was whether a method that does not depend on reaching or speech could reveal earlier knowledge.

## The approach

The method rests on looking time. An infant is shown an event repeatedly until looking drops, and then shown a test event that is either possible or impossible. A longer look at the impossible event is taken as a sign that the infant expected the other outcome.

The steps were:

1. Show an infant a physical event, such as a screen moving to hide an object.
2. Measure how long the infant looks as the event is repeated, until looking time falls.
3. Show a test event that is either possible, with the object behind the barrier, or impossible, with the object apparently passing through the barrier.
4. Compare looking times between the two test events.

Step 3 is the key contrast. The possible and impossible events are matched for visual content, so a difference in looking reflects expectation rather than novelty of appearance.

## Results

The paper reports that five-month-old infants looked longer at the impossible event than at the possible one. The summary used for this page gives this qualitatively. Check the full paper before quoting specific looking times.

## Limitations

- Looking time is an indirect measure, and a longer look could reflect surprise, interest or a difference in visual features.
- Results depend on the details of each display, and small changes can alter the outcome.
- Later work has debated how to interpret these looks, so the claim about knowledge depends on the method's assumptions.

## Open questions

- **What do looking-time differences mean?** Surprise, novelty and other factors can all produce longer looks, and the field is still refining its controls.
- **Does early expectation predict later reasoning?** Links between early looking patterns and later abilities are not yet clear.
