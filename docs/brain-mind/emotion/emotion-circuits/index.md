# Emotion Circuits in the Brain (LeDoux 2000)

LeDoux (2000) reviewed the circuits in the brain that detect threat and produce fear responses, with the amygdala at the centre. Sensory input reaches the amygdala by a fast route, and an output path drives the autonomic and behavioural responses. The review set out a framework that has guided work on fear conditioning ever since, and it made the amygdala the best-studied hub of emotional learning.

This page is part of [Emotion and Affective Computing](../index.md). The parent page gives the overview; this page covers the method and its evidence.

## The problem

Fear responses can be learned quickly, and they can be recalled long after the original event. The question was which brain pathways carry the threat signal, and where the learning that links a stimulus to a threat takes place. Earlier accounts combined many structures without a clear picture of how they connect.

## The approach

The review draws on animal studies of fear conditioning, in which a neutral stimulus is paired with an aversive one. It traces the sensory inputs to the amygdala, the connections within it, and the outputs that drive responses. It then proposes a circuit that accounts for the conditioned response.

The steps of the proposed circuit are:

1. Sensory information about a stimulus reaches the lateral amygdala, both directly from the thalamus and through cortex.
2. Learning within the amygdala links the stimulus to the threat, so that the stimulus alone later triggers a response.
3. The central amygdala sends output to brainstem and hypothalamic structures that drive autonomic and behavioural responses.
4. Cortical pathways modulate these responses, and can refine the appraisal of a threat.

Step 2 is the core of the account. Fear learning is plastic, and it depends on changes in the amygdala's inputs.

## Results

The review reports that the amygdala is necessary for conditioned fear, and that it receives sensory input that can carry a threat signal before cortical processing is complete. The findings are summarised qualitatively. Check the full text before quoting specific results.

## Limitations

- The circuit is built mostly from rodent studies, and its direct application to human emotion needs separate evidence.
- The amygdala is not the only structure involved, and its role in other emotions is less clear.
- The review describes fear more clearly than other emotional states.

## Open questions

- **Do humans share the same threat circuit?** Human imaging supports a role for the amygdala, but the detailed pathways are harder to resolve.
- **How do cortical appraisals change the fast route?** The modulation of the fast pathway by cortex is still being mapped.

## References

### Seminal papers

- LeDoux, J. E. (2000). Emotion circuits in the brain. *Annual Review of Neuroscience*. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.23.1.155 — Reviews the amygdala-centred circuits for fear and threat.

### Surveys and reviews

- Lindquist, K. A., Wager, T. D., Kober, H., Bliss-Moreau, E. & Barrett, L. F. (2012). The brain basis of emotion: a meta-analytic review. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X11000446 — A meta-analysis that questions whether each emotion has a dedicated circuit.
- Russell, J. A. (1980). A circumplex model of affect. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077714 — The dimensional model of affect that the threat signal maps onto.
- Ekman, P. (1992). An argument for basic emotions. *Cognition and Emotion*. https://doi.org/10.1080/02699939208411068 — The basic-emotion view, which the circuit account tests for fear.
- Picard, R. W. (1997). *Affective Computing*. MIT Press. https://www.schweitzer-online.de/en/buch/Picard/Affective-Computing/9780262161701/A39598287/ — Sets out the field of machines that recognise emotion, which does not depend on the detail of these circuits.
