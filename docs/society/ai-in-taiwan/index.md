# AI in Taiwan: Policy, Industry and Research

Taiwan has a national AI strategy and, since December 2025, a basic law for AI. This page covers how those policies are organised, what they require, and where the questions are still open. It concentrates on policy, because the policy documents are the most checkable sources, and it links to the wider topics for industry and research.

## Overview

Taiwan's approach has two layers. A strategy sets goals for industry, talent and public services, and it is updated in plans with fixed periods. A basic law sets principles and duties that apply across sectors, and leaves detailed rules to the agencies responsible for each field. The strategy came first, and the law followed.

## Key concepts

- **AI Action Plan**: a government plan with goals for industry, talent and social use of AI. The current version, AI Action Plan 2.0, covers 2023 to 2026.
- **AI Basic Act**: the law passed by the Legislative Yuan on 23 December 2025 and promulgated by the President on 14 January 2026. It has 20 articles and takes effect on promulgation (Article 20).
- **Competent authority**: the agency responsible for carrying out a law. For the AI Basic Act, the central competent authority is the National Science and Technology Council (NSTC) (Article 2).
- **Risk-based duties**: obligations that depend on how risky a use of AI is. The Act requires government agencies to assess risk and plan mitigation before using AI to carry out duties or provide services (Article 19), and requires AI products or systems designated as high-risk to carry a clear notice or warning (Article 5, paragraph 2).

## How it works

Three layers shape AI policy in Taiwan.

1. **Strategy.** The AI Action Plan 2.0 sets economic and social targets. Its stated aim is to raise the value of Taiwan's AI industry to over NT$250 billion, and it names talent, global reach and the use of AI for labour shortages and an ageing population.
2. **Basic law.** Article 1 states the Act's purpose as building a smart nation and protecting people's fundamental rights while advancing human-centred AI development. Article 4 lists the principles for government promotion of AI, including respect for human autonomy and the robustness and safety of systems. Its duties are described above.
3. **Sector rules.** The Action Plan notes that more laws are planned for sectors such as medicine, finance and transportation. The detail of those rules sits with the sector agencies, not in the basic law.

## Responses

| Instrument | What it does | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| AI Action Plan 2.0 | Sets goals and budgets for industry, talent and public services | A plan has fixed goals and dates; it is not binding in the way a law is |
| AI Basic Act | Sets principles and risk-based duties across sectors | Detailed rules depend on later sector laws and agency guidance |
| AI evaluation centre | Assessment of AI systems, planned under the Ministry of Digital Affairs in the Action Plan | Its scope and methods are set by later decisions |
| Cross-agency talks on AI content | Address AI-generated content and ethics, with rules for AI-generated television content under existing broadcasting laws | Outcomes are not yet described in the sources used here |

## Applications

- **Public services** that must now be assessed for risk before AI is used to deliver them.
- **Industry** that builds AI products and must mark high-risk systems with warnings.
- **Training and talent programmes** that the Action Plan funds to meet its skills targets.

## Open questions

- How will the risk assessments required of government agencies be checked, and by whom?
- Which sectors will get detailed rules, and how quickly will they arrive?
- How will the basic law's principle of protecting fundamental rights be applied when the sector laws are still unwritten?
