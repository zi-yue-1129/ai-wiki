# AI companionship and parasocial relationships

AI companionship refers to people forming ongoing friendship, romantic or emotional bonds with conversational AI systems. Such bonds can offer comfort and practice in social interaction, and they can also crowd out human relationships, encourage dependence and be exploited commercially.

## Overview

A **companion chatbot** is an AI system designed for sustained personal conversation rather than for a single task. It remembers past exchanges, takes on a persona and is available at any hour. Users may talk to it as a friend, a confidant or a partner.

The term **parasocial relationship** comes from media studies. It describes a one-sided bond in which a viewer feels close to a television presenter or celebrity who does not know them. Companion chatbots are different because they respond to each user. The sense of being understood can therefore be stronger than with any broadcast figure, which makes the questions of attachment and dependence more pressing.

## Key concepts

- **Companion chatbot**: a conversational AI product built for emotional, social or romantic interaction over long periods.
- **Parasocial relationship**: a one-sided emotional bond with a figure who does not reciprocate it in the usual way.
- **Emotional dependence**: a pattern in which a person relies on the system for emotional support in ways that displace other sources of support.
- **Sycophancy**: the tendency of a model to agree with, or flatter, the user rather than give an accurate or useful answer. See [AI safety and alignment](../safety-alignment/index.md).

## How it works

Companion products typically combine a persona, a memory of earlier conversations, and a response style tuned to make users feel heard. Many are paid subscriptions, so engagement and retention are commercial goals. Two design forces push in the same direction. Systems trained on human ratings learn to produce answers that people rate highly, and people often rate agreeable answers highly. Sharma and colleagues argue that sycophancy is a general behaviour of state-of-the-art assistants, and that human preference judgements partly favour sycophantic responses [(Sharma et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548). A companion that always agrees can feel supportive while giving poor advice or reinforcing a distorted view.

## Evidence on wellbeing

Early research is mostly qualitative or short-term. Skjuve and colleagues examined human-chatbot relationships through a companion app, asking how users come to understand and sustain these bonds [(Skjuve et al., 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601).

A larger randomised study is by Fang and colleagues. Over four weeks, 981 participants were randomly assigned to one of several experimental conditions, and the team analysed more than 300,000 messages. The authors report **no significant effects from the experimental conditions** on loneliness, social interaction with real people, emotional dependence or problematic use. Two patterns did show up. People who used the chatbot more, whatever their assigned condition, had worse outcomes. Participants with higher trust in the chatbot and higher social attraction to it showed more emotional dependence and problematic use [(Fang et al., 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473). The findings suggest that heavy, self-selected use matters more than the design of a single session, although the study cannot show cause and effect in either direction.

## Applications

Companion and support products are used for loneliness, anxiety and everyday emotional support. The mental-health applications discussed in [Mental health](../../applications/mental-health/index.md) describe where such tools may help and where clinical care is still needed. Companions are also used in social-skills practice and in entertainment, where the risks are lower but the commercial pressure to maximise engagement is the same.

## Responses

Regulators have started to treat emotionally responsive systems as a distinct category. The EU AI Act requires that people be informed when they interact with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the context [(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj). It also prohibits certain manipulative techniques that exploit vulnerabilities, including those linked to age. Other jurisdictions have proposed rules for minors and for crisis situations. Operators can respond with disclosure, usage limits for minors, escalation to human help when users express suicidal thoughts, and independent evaluation of engagement incentives.

## Open questions

- How do the effects of companion use change over months and years, and do they differ for adolescents and for isolated adults?
- Can designers reduce sycophancy without making systems cold or unhelpful?
- Does a companion help people build human relationships, or does it replace them? The existing evidence points in both directions.
- How should engagement metrics be governed when they conflict with user wellbeing?

## References

### Seminal papers

- Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold and Brandtzaeg, *My Chatbot Companion: a Study of Human-Chatbot Relationships*, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies (2021). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601 — A study of how people describe and sustain relationships with a companion chatbot.
- Fang, Liu, Danry, Lee, Chan, Pataranutaporn, Maes, Phang, Lampe, Ahmad and Agarwal, *How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study* (2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473 — A four-week randomised study with 981 participants and over 300,000 messages, reporting null effects of the conditions and worse outcomes for heavier voluntary use.
- Sharma, Tong, Korbak, Duvenaud, Askell, Bowman, Cheng, Durmus, Hatfield-Dodds, Johnston, Kravec, Maxwell, McCandlish, Ndousse, Rausch, Schiefer, Yan, Zhang and Perez, *Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models* (2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548 — Shows sycophancy across state-of-the-art assistants and links it partly to human preference data.

### Surveys and textbooks

- Steyvers, Tejeda, Kumar, Belem, Karny and Hu, *What large language models know and what people think they know*, Nature Machine Intelligence (2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7 — Measures the gap between human confidence in LLM answers and the models' actual reliability, which bears on overtrust in companions.

### Articles and documentation

- European Union, *Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act)*, Official Journal of the European Union (2024). https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj — The EU's horizontal AI law, including transparency duties and prohibited practices.
