# AI literacy and public understanding

AI literacy is the set of competencies that lets people understand what AI systems can and cannot do, judge their output, and use them responsibly. Public understanding of AI shapes how people trust these systems, and it shapes how much say they have in how AI is governed.

## Overview

Most people meet AI through products rather than through its technical workings. A search ranking, a voice assistant and a chatbot can all rely on machine learning, yet users rarely know which part is learned from data, which part is a rule, and how confident the system should be. Literacy means being able to ask those questions. It does not require programming, though it does require a working model of where AI errs.

## Key concepts

- **AI literacy**: competencies for understanding, evaluating and using AI, including knowing its limits.
- **Mental model**: a person's internal picture of how a system works. A model can be accurate, partial or misleading.
- **Calibration gap**: the difference between how confident people are in a system's answers and how often those answers are right.
- **Algorithmic awareness**: knowing that a service uses automated decisions, and what those decisions affect.

## How it works

AI literacy is built through education, product design and public communication, and these three routes reinforce each other. Education gives learners concepts and the habit of questioning outputs. Design can make a system's uncertainty and sources visible. Public communication shapes the expectations people bring to a product before they use it.

Research on how people build mental models of language models points to a central problem. Steyvers and colleagues compare how confident people are in LLM answers with how reliable those answers are. They describe a **calibration gap** and a **discrimination gap**, which measures how well people tell correct answers from incorrect ones [(Steyvers et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7). A literate user is one whose trust tracks reliability, not fluency.

## Frameworks and curricula

Long and Magerko propose a set of AI literacy competencies together with design considerations for learning systems, grounding the concept in what a person needs to know to interact with AI [(Long and Magerko, 2020)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376727). Ng and colleagues carry out an exploratory review of how the concept of AI literacy has been conceptualised [(Ng et al., 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2021.100041). A scoping review by Laupichler and colleagues maps the literature on teaching AI literacy in higher and adult education [(Laupichler et al., 2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2022.100101).

Regulation has started to include literacy as a duty. Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff and other people who operate systems on their behalf [(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj).

## Applications

Literacy programmes are taught in schools, universities and adult education. Public agencies and companies publish explainers and product notices. Newsrooms and fact-checkers use similar skills when they assess AI-generated content, which is covered in [Misinformation and deepfakes](../misinformation/index.md).

## Open questions

- Which competencies matter most for ordinary users, and how can they be measured without testing knowledge of trivia?
- Does explaining how AI works reduce overtrust, or does it sometimes increase it?
- How should literacy be taught to adults who never studied the underlying mathematics?
- How much of the responsibility for literacy belongs to providers, schools and users?

- Long and Magerko, *What is AI Literacy? Competencies and Design Considerations*, CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (2020). https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376727 — Defines AI literacy through competencies and gives design considerations for teaching it.
