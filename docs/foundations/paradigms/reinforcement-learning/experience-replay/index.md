# Experience Replay

Experience replay stores the transitions an agent has collected in a buffer and reuses them for later updates. It is what makes off-policy value learning practical: a mini-batch drawn from the buffer contains past experience, so updates do not depend only on the newest step. It also breaks the strong correlation between consecutive transitions, which stabilises training with neural networks. Replay is a component of [value-based methods](../value-based-methods/index.md) and of most off-policy actor–critic methods, and is usually absent from purely on-policy policy-gradient methods.

## Overview

A replay buffer holds tuples \((s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})\) produced by a behaviour policy. At each update the learner samples a batch, computes a loss on the sampled transitions, and continues. Three decisions define any variant:

- **Which transitions are kept**: all recent ones (a first-in, first-out buffer), or a selection.
- **How they are sampled**: uniformly, by priority, or as whole sequences.
- **How the data is corrected**: because stored transitions were generated by older policies, sampling can introduce bias that needs compensating.

## Approaches

| Variant | Idea | Key paper |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform replay | Sample transitions uniformly from a fixed-size FIFO buffer | Mnih et al. (2015) |
| Prioritised replay | Sample with probability growing with the TD error, and correct the bias with importance weights | Schaul et al. (2015), arXiv:1511.05952 |
| Hindsight replay (HER) | Relabel failed transitions in goal-conditioned tasks with the goal that was actually reached | Andrychowicz et al. (2017), arXiv:1707.01495 |
| Sequence replay | Store fixed-length sequences with the recurrent state at their start, and use a burn-in prefix to rebuild that state | Kapturowski et al. (2019), ICLR |
| Distributed replay | Many actors feed one shared prioritised buffer, while a single learner samples from it | Horgan et al. (2018), arXiv:1803.00933 |
| Capacity and ratio studies | Measure how buffer size and the number of updates per collected transition change learning | Fedus et al. (2020), arXiv:2007.06700 |

### Uniform replay

The original deep Q-network used uniform sampling from a buffer of recent transitions. Uniform sampling is simple and unbiased with respect to the stored data, but it spends updates equally on transitions the agent already predicts well and on rare, surprising ones.

### Prioritised replay

Prioritised replay gives each transition a priority, usually the magnitude of its TD error, and samples in proportion to a power of that priority. High-error transitions are replayed more often, which speeds learning. Because the sampling distribution is no longer the data distribution, each update is weighted with importance-sampling weights. Priorities are stale once the network changes, and computing them for a large buffer needs an efficient data structure such as a sum tree.

### Hindsight replay

In goal-conditioned tasks most episodes end in failure, so they give little learning signal. Hindsight replay treats the state the agent did reach as if it had been the intended goal, and stores the transition with that relabelled goal and a reward of success. The method turns failures into useful examples for the goal the agent actually achieved.

### Sequence and distributed replay

Recurrent networks need sequences rather than single transitions. Recurrent replay stores fixed-length segments together with the stored hidden state and uses a burn-in prefix so that the state is not stale when training starts. Distributed replay scales this up: many actors generate experience in parallel, and the buffer keeps priorities computed by the actors.

## Trade-offs

- **Bias versus speed.** Prioritisation learns faster but changes the training distribution. Importance weights correct the expectation but can have high variance.
- **Staleness.** Old transitions come from older policies. Large buffers help sample efficiency but make the data less representative of the current policy.
- **Memory.** A buffer of raw observations, especially images, can dominate memory use.
- **Hyperparameters.** Capacity, the ratio of updates to new data, and the priority exponent interact, so results from one setting often do not transfer.

## Connections

Replay is closely linked to [exploration](../exploration/index.md), since a larger buffer lets an agent revisit rare successes. The same problem of keeping old data while learning new tasks appears in [continual learning](../../continual/replay-and-memory/index.md), where replay is used to prevent forgetting.

The brain comparison is one of the best-known in computational neuroscience. During sleep and quiet rest, hippocampal place cells replay sequences of recent experience, sometimes in reverse order. Foster and Wilson (2006) reported reverse replay in awake rats, which resembles prioritised sampling of reward-related sequences. The analogy is suggestive rather than a demonstrated mechanism; see [memory and learning](../../../../brain-mind/memory-learning/index.md).

## Open issues and further variants

- **How long to keep data.** A FIFO buffer forgets, while a reservoir keeps old transitions. Which retention rule works best in changing environments is unsettled.
- **Priorities that go stale.** Priorities are computed when a transition is stored, so they lag behind the network. Recomputing them costs time, and approximate schemes trade accuracy for speed.
- **Learned sampling.** Choosing which transitions to replay can itself be learned, for example by a meta-level policy. This is less mature than hand-designed priorities.
- **Off-policy correction.** Importance weights can explode for distant policies. Other corrections, such as truncating traces or clipping ratios, exist but interact with the replay design.
- **Replay without storage.** Generative models can replay past experience without storing it, which matters for privacy and memory limits, but generated samples may drift.
- **Off-policy data for large models.** Replay-like reuse of demonstrations and model outputs is increasingly used in training large models, but the theory for these settings is incomplete.

### Seminal papers

- Mnih, V., et al. (2015). "Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning". *Nature* 518. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature14236 — The deep Q-network, which popularised uniform replay with a target network.
- Schaul, T., et al. (2015). "Prioritized Experience Replay". arXiv:1511.05952 — Samples transitions by TD error and corrects the bias with importance weights.
- Andrychowicz, M., et al. (2017). "Hindsight Experience Replay". arXiv:1707.01495 — Relabels failed goal-conditioned episodes with the goals that were reached.
- Horgan, D., et al. (2018). "Distributed Prioritized Experience Replay". arXiv:1803.00933 — Many actors share one prioritised buffer, scaling replay to large numbers of environments.
- Kapturowski, S., et al. (2019). "Recurrent Experience Replay in Distributed Reinforcement Learning". ICLR. https://iclr.cc/virtual/2019/poster/648 — Stores recurrent sequences with stored hidden states and burn-in.

### Surveys and textbooks

- Fedus, W., et al. (2020). "Revisiting Fundamentals of Experience Replay". arXiv:2007.06700 — Studies how buffer capacity and the replay ratio change the results of value-based agents.
