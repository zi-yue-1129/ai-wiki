# Benchmark Contamination

Benchmark contamination occurs when test items, or close copies of them, appear in a model's training data. A model that has seen the answers can score well by recall, so a reported gain may reflect the training corpus more than the ability the benchmark was built to measure. The problem is hard to rule out for closed models, and it is one reason the field has moved toward dynamic and private test sets.

## Overview

Language models are trained on large web scrapes, and benchmarks are published on the same web. Test questions appear in papers, blog posts and code repositories, and each copy is a chance for the text to enter a crawl. Dodge et al. (2021) found evaluation examples from other NLP benchmarks in the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus, which shows that the problem is structural rather than the fault of careless authors.

Benchmark scores rank models and guide decisions, so an inflated score can place a weaker model above a stronger one. The [evaluation overview](index.md) lists contamination among its pitfalls, and [Benchmark Suites](benchmark-suites.md) covers saturation, where even small leaks can decide a ranking.

## Key concepts

- **Contamination**: test items, verbatim or paraphrased, present in training data.
- **Memorisation**: a model reproducing a training sequence rather than generalising from it. It is the mechanism by which contamination inflates scores.
- **Inflation**: the gap between a model's score on leaked items and its score on unseen items of similar difficulty.
- **Decontamination**: removing test-like text from training data before training, usually by matching word sequences.
- **Canary string**: a unique, random marker placed in a document. It can ask crawlers to skip the document, or reveal later whether the document was used in training.
- **Dynamic benchmark**: a test whose items are refreshed over time, so newer models face items they could not have seen.
- **Private benchmark**: a test whose items are withheld from public release.

## How it works

The most direct route is a copy of the test set in the crawl. Less obvious routes include worked solutions posted after publication, and synthetic training data generated by a model that has already seen the benchmark (see [Synthetic Data](../synthetic-data/index.md)).

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Benchmark published online] --> B[Copied into a web crawl]
  B --> C[Model pretrained on the crawl]
  C --> D[Model evaluated on the same items]
  D --> E[Score reflects recall]
  E --> F[Inflated comparison]
```

Memorisation depends on how often a text appears. Carlini et al. (2022) found that memorisation grows with model size, with the number of times an example is duplicated, and with the amount of context used to prompt the model. Lee et al. (2021) showed that common corpora contain many near-duplicates, and that removing them reduced verbatim output and train–test overlap.

### How contamination inflates scores

Contamination inflates scores when leaked items are answered from memory rather than skill, but the size of the effect varies by benchmark. In GPT-3's analysis, potential contamination exceeded 50 percent for about a quarter of benchmarks, yet most scores changed little on the clean subsets. The authors concluded either that their conservative filter overestimated contamination or that contamination had little effect.[^gpt3] A few groups, such as word scrambling and reading comprehension, were flagged for closer study.

Decontamination is also harder than it looks. GPT-3's own attempt to remove overlapping text only partly succeeded because of a bug, and retraining was too costly to repeat (Brown et al., 2020). A filter that finds no n-gram overlap does not prove a benchmark is clean (Yang et al., 2023). Sainz et al. (2023) therefore argue that contamination should be measured separately for each benchmark.

## Key methods

Detection methods differ in what they need: some require the training corpus, which closed models do not publish, while others need only outputs or token probabilities. They also answer different questions. Was a text in the corpus, or has the model memorised it?

| Method | What it needs | What it can show | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| n-gram overlap | Access to the corpus | Shared word sequences between test set and corpus | Misses paraphrase and translation (Yang et al., 2023) |
| Canary strings and copyright traps | Control over inserted text | Whether planted text was learned | Says nothing about benchmarks that were never marked |
| Likelihood tests | Token probabilities | Unusually predictable test items | Membership signals barely beat chance on LLMs (Duan et al., 2024) |
| Ordering test | Likelihoods of shuffled item orders | Provable contamination in black-box models | Needs an exchangeable benchmark with enough items |
| Slot guessing | Prompted outputs | Model fills a hidden part of a test item | Depends on the model completing the item |

**n-gram overlap** is the most practical method. GPT-3's authors flagged test items with a 13-gram match against the training set and rescored a "clean" subset without them (Brown et al., 2020). Its weakness is that it looks only for shared strings.

**Canary strings** are unique sequences placed in a document. Carlini et al. (2018) inserted random sequences into training data to measure how strongly a model memorises rare text. Meeus et al. (2024) applied the idea to copyright, inserting fictitious entries into books. Medium-length traps repeated 100 times were not detectable, while longer sequences repeated many times were, so a marker must be strong enough to be memorised.

**Likelihood tests** ask whether the model finds a passage unusually predictable. Li (2023) estimated benchmark contamination from perplexity without the full corpus, reporting signs of memorisation in reading-comprehension and summarisation benchmarks and less in multiple-choice ones. Shi et al. (2023) proposed Min-K% Prob, which looks at the least likely tokens in a passage. Duan et al. (2024) found that membership inference on Pile-trained models barely outperforms random guessing, so these signals remain uncertain.

**The ordering test** rests on a simple observation: for exchangeable items, a clean model should find every ordering equally likely, while a contaminated model tends to prefer the canonical order. Oren et al. (2023) turned this into a statistical test that needs only black-box likelihoods, and audited five public models without finding pervasive contamination.

**Slot guessing** hides part of a test item and asks the model to fill it in. Deng et al. (2023) combined this with retrieval-based searches of pretraining corpora. A correct guess of hidden text suggests that the item was seen.

### Preventing contamination

Three approaches are common, and each has costs.

- **Decontaminated training data**: filter or deduplicate the corpus against known test sets before training. It catches only the text that has been identified, and it is only as good as its matching method.
- **Dynamic benchmarks**: LiveCodeBench collects coding problems published over time, so each model can be tested on problems released after its training cut-off (Jain et al., 2024). Dynamic benchmarks need continuous curation, and scores on different item sets are harder to compare.
- **Private or encrypted test data**: Jacovi et al. (2023) propose publishing test data encrypted with a public key and licensed against derivative use, which keeps the items out of plain-text crawls. Private sets are safer from leakage, but outside groups cannot inspect them, which limits reproducibility.

## Connections

- **Benchmarks.** Contamination is one of the validity threats in [Evaluation & Benchmarks](index.md), and it applies to the suites discussed in [Benchmark Suites](benchmark-suites.md), such as MMLU and HumanEval.
- **Privacy.** The same mechanisms, memorisation and likelihood tests, are used to protect people rather than to score models. [Membership Inference](../../society/privacy/membership-inference.md) and [Training Data Extraction](../../society/privacy/training-data-extraction.md) cover the privacy side.
- **Data curation.** Decontamination relies on the near-duplicate detection used for web corpora; see [MinHash Deduplication](../data/minhash-deduplication.md).
- **Agents.** Agent benchmarks face the same question when their tasks come from public repositories; see [SWE-bench](../agents/agent-evaluation/swe-bench/index.md).
- **Psychology.** Human testing has an analogous problem: a student who has seen the exam beforehand is scored partly on recall of that exam. See [Psychology & AI](../../brain-mind/psychology/index.md).

## Applications

- **Reporting.** Labs can publish their decontamination methods and clean-subset scores, as GPT-3 did, so that readers can judge the effect.
- **Independent audits.** Tests that need only model outputs let outside researchers check closed models, as in Oren et al. (2023).
- **Time-stamped evaluation.** Benchmarks that add items after each model's cut-off reduce the chance that a model has seen the test items (Jain et al., 2024).

## Open questions

- Membership signals barely beat chance on large models (Duan et al., 2024). What reliable evidence can a closed model's outputs alone provide?
- Do detectors survive rewording? N-gram and likelihood methods can miss paraphrases (Yang et al., 2023).
- Once a dynamic benchmark is published, its items enter the web. How long does an item stay uncontaminated?

[^gpt3]: Brown et al. (2020), section 4, listed in References. The paper reports that potential contamination exceeded 50 percent for about a quarter of the benchmarks it checked.

## References

### Seminal papers

- Brown, T. B. et al. (2020). *Language Models are Few-Shot Learners*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165 — The GPT-3 paper, whose evaluation section flags benchmark overlap with the training data and rescores clean subsets.
- Carlini, N. et al. (2018). *The Secret Sharer: Evaluating and Testing Unintended Memorization in Neural Networks*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232 — Inserts rare random sequences into training data to measure how strongly a model memorises them.
- Carlini, N. et al. (2022). *Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646 — Shows that memorisation grows with model size, duplication and prompt context.
- Lee, K. et al. (2021). *Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.06499 — Shows that near-duplicates in common corpora drive verbatim output and that train–test overlap is common in standard datasets.
- Dodge, J. et al. (2021). *Documenting Large Webtext Corpora: A Case Study on the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08758 — Documents C4 and reports evaluation examples from other benchmarks in its text.

### Related work

- Deng, C. et al. (2023). *Investigating Data Contamination in Modern Benchmarks for Large Language Models*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783 — Introduces retrieval-based overlap search and the slot-guessing protocol for models whose training data is hidden.
- Yang, S. et al. (2023). *Rethinking Benchmark and Contamination for Language Models with Rephrased Samples*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04850 — Shows that paraphrased and translated test data can bypass n-gram decontamination.
- Oren, Y. et al. (2023). *Proving Test Set Contamination in Black Box Language Models*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623 — Proposes an exchangeability test on benchmark item orders and audits five public models.
- Sainz, O. et al. (2023). *NLP Evaluation in trouble: On the Need to Measure LLM Data Contamination for each Benchmark*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018 — Position paper arguing that contamination must be measured for each benchmark because it inflates scores.
- Li, Y. (2023). *Estimating Contamination via Perplexity: Quantifying Memorisation in Language Model Evaluation*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10677 — Estimates benchmark contamination from perplexity without access to the full training set.
- Shi, W. et al. (2023). *Detecting Pretraining Data from Large Language Models*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789 — Introduces Min-K% Prob, a detector for pretraining data that needs no reference model.
- Duan, M. et al. (2024). *Do Membership Inference Attacks Work on Large Language Models?*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841 — Finds membership inference barely outperforms random guessing on models trained on the Pile.
- Jacovi, A. et al. (2023). *Stop Uploading Test Data in Plain Text: Practical Strategies for Mitigating Data Contamination by Evaluation Benchmarks*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10160 — Proposes encrypting public test data and licensing it against derivative use.
- Jain, N. et al. (2024). *LiveCodeBench: Holistic and Contamination Free Evaluation of Large Language Models for Code*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974 — Builds a code benchmark from contest problems published over time.
- Meeus, M. et al. (2024). *Copyright Traps for Large Language Models*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09363 — Inserts fictitious entries into copyrighted text and tests when they can be detected.
