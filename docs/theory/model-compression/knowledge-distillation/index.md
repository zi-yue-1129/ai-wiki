# Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation trains a small **student** model to reproduce the behaviour of a large **teacher** model. The student learns from the teacher's output probabilities or internal representations rather than from the labels alone, so it can often reach accuracy that the same architecture would not reach when trained directly on the data.

## Overview

Distillation is one family of [model compression](../index.md). Unlike [quantisation](../quantisation/index.md) and [pruning](../pruning/index.md), it does not modify a large model in place. It produces a new, smaller model, and the student can have a different architecture from the teacher.

The idea rests on the observation that a trained network's output distribution carries more information than its top prediction. If a classifier gives a dog image a small probability of being a cat and a tiny probability of being a truck, those relative values reveal how the classes are related. Hinton et al. (2015) called this information **dark knowledge**.

## Key concepts

- **Soft targets.** Teacher outputs are softened with a temperature \(T\) before comparison, so that small probabilities are not lost in the noise: \(p_i = \exp(z_i/T) / \sum_j \exp(z_j/T)\).
- **Temperature.** Higher \(T\) spreads the probabilities, giving the student more information about the relations between classes. The loss is usually scaled by \(T^2\) to keep gradient sizes comparable.
- **Response-based versus feature-based.** Response-based distillation matches output distributions. Feature-based distillation matches intermediate representations, so the student learns how the teacher computes as well as what it outputs.
- **Sequence-level distillation.** For language models, the teacher generates complete outputs and the student is trained on them as ordinary supervised data. This is the most common form for large language models today.
- **Teacher quality.** A student is bounded by what the teacher knows. A weak teacher, or a teacher with biased outputs, passes its errors to the student.

## How it works

Hinton et al. combine two losses. The student is trained on the true labels with a standard cross-entropy term, and on the teacher's softened outputs with a KL divergence term. A weight \(\alpha\) balances the two:

\[
\mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha)\, \mathrm{CE}(y, \sigma(z_s)) + \alpha\, T^2\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\sigma(z_t/T)\,\Vert\,\sigma(z_s/T)\right)
\]

where \(z_s\) and \(z_t\) are the student and teacher logits and \(\sigma\) is the softmax. The teacher is frozen, so its outputs can be precomputed or generated on the fly.

For a language model, the same idea is used at the level of tokens, or of whole sequences generated by the teacher. Distilled models often train on far more data than the original teacher saw in its supervised stage, because the teacher can label or generate it cheaply.

## Comparison of families

| Family | What the student matches | Typical methods | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Response-based | Output distributions, with the temperature-softened teacher | Hinton distillation, DistilBERT, teacher assistant | [Response-based](response-based/index.md) |
| Feature-based | Intermediate activations or attention maps | FitNets, attention transfer | [Feature-based](feature-based/index.md) |
| Relation-based | Relations between examples in a batch | RKD, similarity-preserving | [Relation-based](relation-based/index.md) |
| Self-distillation | The network's own earlier copies or deeper layers | Born-again networks, Be Your Own Teacher | [Self-distillation](self-distillation/index.md) |
| Data-free | Teacher outputs on synthetic or generated inputs | Zero-shot KD, sequence-level KD | [Data-free](data-free/index.md) |
| LLM distillation | Generated text, the student's own samples, or reasoning traces | Sequence-level KD, MiniLLM, DeepSeek-R1 | [LLM distillation](llm-distillation/index.md) |

Response-based distillation is the baseline and the most common in practice. The other families add signal from the teacher's internals, relations, or generated data, at the cost of more design choices and more compute.

## Trade-offs

- **Size versus quality.** Smaller students lose capability, especially on tasks that need the teacher's broader knowledge.
- **Cost of the teacher.** Distillation needs many teacher forward passes. The cost is paid once and then amortised over every use of the student.
- **Architecture freedom versus transfer.** A student may use a faster architecture than the teacher, but the transfer of knowledge is harder when the two differ greatly.
- **Licensing and data.** Using a proprietary model's outputs to train a student may be restricted by the teacher's terms of use, which varies by provider.

## Connections

Distillation is a training technique, so it sits between the [training](../../../engineering/training/index.md) pages and the [MLOps](../../../engineering/mlops/index.md) pages that choose which model to serve. The [large language model](../../models/llm/index.md) page lists distillation among the methods for reducing cost and latency.

The idea of learning from a teacher's graded judgements, rather than from hard answers, has parallels in human learning. Teachers, parents and peers give graded feedback that carries information beyond the right answer. Whether the brain uses an analogous mechanism is an open question; see [cognitive science](../../../brain-mind/cognitive-science/index.md).

## Applications

- **Mobile and embedded models**, where a distilled student replaces a large model in a product.
- **Language model families**, where smaller models are distilled from a larger sibling to serve as cheaper options.
- **Reasoning models**, where traces produced by a stronger model are used to train smaller models to follow the same steps.
- **Speech and vision systems**, where distillation from an ensemble or a large model improves a deployed model without changing its architecture.

## Open questions

- **What knowledge transfers.** Students match outputs more easily than internal reasoning. How to transfer the process by which a teacher solves a problem is not settled.
- **Teacher limits.** A student cannot exceed its teacher, but the choice of teacher and data strongly affects the student's quality. Principles for choosing them are limited.
- **Self-distillation.** Models that distil from their own earlier versions can improve, but why this works is not fully explained.
- **Evaluation.** A student may match the teacher on benchmarks while differing in calibration, robustness or failure modes.

## References

### Seminal papers

- Hinton, G., Vinyals, O., Dean, J. (2015). *Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network*. arXiv:1503.02531. https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531 — Introduces soft targets and temperature. The abstract describes compressing an ensemble's knowledge into a single model that is easier to deploy.
- Sanh, V., Debut, L., Chaumond, J., Wolf, T. (2019). *DistilBERT, a Distilled Version of BERT: Smaller, Faster, Cheaper and Lighter*. arXiv:1910.01108. https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01108 — A distilled BERT that keeps 97% of the teacher's language understanding, at 40% smaller size and 60% faster.
- Romero, A., et al. (2014). *FitNets: Hints for Thin Deep Nets*. arXiv:1412.6550. https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6550 — Trains a thin, deep student on intermediate hints. On CIFAR-10, a student with almost 10.4 times fewer parameters outperforms a larger, state-of-the-art teacher.
- Kim, Y., Rush, A. M. (2016). *Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation*. arXiv:1606.07947. https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07947 — Distils sequence models by training on teacher outputs, giving a student about 10 times faster than its teacher with little loss in performance.

### Surveys and textbooks

- Gou, J., et al. (2021). *Knowledge Distillation: A Survey*. International Journal of Computer Vision, 129, 1789–1819. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11263-021-01453-z — Surveys distillation by knowledge type, training scheme, architecture and application.
